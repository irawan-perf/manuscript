\begin{abstract}
Current development of microservice architectures heavily relies on API gateways and high-performance RPC, such as gRPC. It aims to connect 
external RESTful traffic with internal gRPC communications. However, typical protocol translation at the application layer often experiences the issue of the \textit{connection-per-request} (CPR) anti-pattern. This emerges when gateways continually create and disassemble network contexts for each request, leading to inefficiencies. This $O(N)$ connection churn causes a severe drop in throughput and triggers non-linear latency spikes, damaging the $O(1)$ multiplexing design of HTTP/2. In this paper, we propose an automated controller framework that operates by calculating and predicting latency spikes at saturation points and dynamically reducing end-to-end latency by enforcing the \textit{Persistent Channel Reuse} (PCR) method across microservice deployments. Our framework automatically provisions an optimized Envoy sidecar proxy at the frontend deployment pods. We formalize this behavior using a queueing-theoretic model that directly links connection churn with worker queue saturation. To evaluate our solution, we executed extensive benchmarks on an isolated production Kubernetes cluster. The experimental results show that our automated Envoy sidecar controller successfully suppresses $p99$ end-to-end latency by up to 81.2\% in the Synchronous framework stack, while increasing throughput by 60.5\% in the Asynchronous framework stack under severe workload stress. This research provides a quantification paradigm of CPU-bound microservices applications and an automated orchestration primitive for high-throughput cloud continuum and Post-5G edge computing environments. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Transport Protocols and Communication Overhead in Microservices}
Microservice architectures increasingly rely on high-performance Remote Procedure Call (RPC) frameworks, such as gRPC, to facilitate low-latency inter-service communication over HTTP/2.  Recent work reports that because gRPC uses HTTP/2 and Protocol Buffers, it can cut latency by about 30\% compared with REST for inter-service communication \cite{alzahrani2026scalable}. That kind of gain matters most when applications make many small synchronous calls, since serialization cost, request framing, and connection handling are paid repeatedly across a request path.   

While HTTP/2 multiplexing significantly reduces connection overhead compared to HTTP/1.1 REST interfaces, system performance remains highly sensitive to underlying transport-layer connection management. Existing benchmark studies demonstrate gRPC's superiority in execution time and resource utilization. Taha \emph{et al.} point out that proactive performance prediction in microservice systems struggles with dynamic dependencies and multiplexing, especially because long and changing call chains add delay \cite{taha2024proactive}. When one service request triggers many downstream calls, queueing and contention on shared channels can make tail latency less predictable even if average latency stays low.

However, these studies predominantly assume persistent channel reuse (PCR) under steady-state workloads. Several aspects significantly influence and characterize the causes of increased tail latency and non-linear throughput degradation in cloud continuum environments—including ephemeral functions, rapid auto-scaling, connection-per-request (CPR) patterns, and connection churn—which frequently lead to the exhaustion of the OS TCP listen backlog. Biondani \emph{et al.} found that gRPC performance degrades for large file transfers, and they attribute this to HTTP/2 transport behavior: over a 45 ms RTT path, the bandwidth-delay product interacts badly with HTTP/2 application-layer flow control, so throughput is throttled while senders wait for WINDOW\_UPDATE frames \cite{biondani2026mate}. This is important for inter-service design because it shows that HTTP/2 multiplexing helps most for many small concurrent exchanges, not for every traffic type.

\subsection{Control Loops and Traffic Management}
The use of autonomic computing paradigms based on the Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute with Knowledge (MAPE-K) feedback loop has become common practice to address performance issues in cloud-native settings \cite{armstrong2017towards}. Traditional implementations integrate autonomic managers into the service mesh or API gateway to handle dynamic autoscaling, circuit breaking, anomaly detection, and traffic activity \cite{carnevali2025compositional}. A recent article introduces machine learning and agentic control units to predict microservice anomalies \cite{toka2021machine}. Nevertheless, these approaches introduce non-negligible telemetry aggregation and decision latencies, making them less effective in responding to immediate network socket saturation \cite{qiu2020firm}. Furthermore, standard load-balancing techniques used in gRPC, such as client-side round-robin or fixed connection age limits, do not effectively address connection starvation problems when runtime conditions suddenly shift between CPR and PCR operational modes \cite{dautov2014utilising}.

\subsection{Positioning and Novel Contributions}
In contrast to existing static performance evaluations or resource-intensive AI-driven controller techniques, our approach introduces a lightweight, mathematically based autonomic controller tailored specifically for gRPC connection management. By clearly establishing the transition boundary between CPR and PCR paradigms through queueing dynamics, the proposed controller achieves sensing and actuation results within milliseconds. It reduces monitoring overhead in the baseline during normal operation, efficiently minimizes end-to-end tail latency ($p99$) across various load categories, and prevents throughput collapse during critical TCP backlog saturation.

\section{Mathematical Modeling of gRPC Connection Management}
\label{sec:math-model}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In order to comprehend the communication bottleneck, it is essential to break down the entire lifecycle of a request as it progresses through the API gateway. This analysis will help us identify the key stages and factors contributing to the bottleneck in the communication process. We model this environment by evaluating two distinct application gateway architectures: a synchronous model \cite{gunicorn} (e.g., Flask thread pools) and an asynchronous model \cite{uvicorn} (e.g., FastAPI event loops).

Let $T_{total}$ be the total end-to-end latency experienced by the client. We define $T_{total}$ as:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{total}} = T_{\text{http}} + W_{\text{queue}} + S_{\text{gateway}} + T_{\text{network}}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{description}
  \item[$T_{\text{http}}$]    : ingress HTTP processing time.
  \item[$W_{\text{queue}}$]   : queueing delay at the gateway's worker processes.
  \item[$S_{\text{gateway}}$] : service time required by the gateway to establish and execute the downstream gRPC call.
  \item[$T_{\text{network}}$] : physical network propagation delay and backend computation time (baseline constant $c \approx 3\ \text{ms}$).
\end{description}

To isolate the backend processing efficiency from the communication overhead, $T_{\text{network}}$ is further decomposed into pure server compute time ($T_{\text{compute}}$) and the combined network transit and backend queueing delay ($T_{\text{net\_queue}}$):

\begin{equation}
	T_{\text{network}} = T_{\text{compute}} + T_{\text{net\_queue}}
\end{equation}

During isolated backend service evaluation, these variables are extracted by aggregating raw container-level telemetry across all active backend pods. The pure compute time is derived from the cumulative execution time ($\sum T_{\text{exec}}$) and the total request count ($\sum N_{\text{req}}$):

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:t-comp}
	T_{\text{compute}} = \left( \frac{\sum T_{\text{exec}}}{\sum N_{\text{req}}} \right) \times 1000 \quad \text{(ms)}
\end{equation}

Subsequently, the network and internal queueing delay is isolated by subtracting the pure compute time from the measured average response latency ($T_{\text{avg}}$):

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:t-net}
	T_{\text{net\_queue}} = T_{\text{avg}} - T_{\text{compute}} \quad \text{(ms)}
\end{equation}

This decomposition proves that pure computation ($T_{\text{compute}}$) remains a fractional baseline constant ($\approx 0.002\ \text{ms}$ in our test environment), isolating gRPC connection handling, payload serialization, and queue saturation as the primary variables. Therefore, the critical variable in our end-to-end study is $S_{gateway}$. We model this variable under two distinct operational paradigms, representing the system state before and after the proposed controller's intervention.

\subsection{Paradigm 1: Connection-Per-Request (CPR)}
\label{sec2.1}

In the Connection-Per-Request (CPR) anti-pattern, as shown in the CPR section of Fig. \ref{fig:seq-model}, the application gateway initializes a new gRPC channel for every incoming request. The service time $S_{cpr}$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    S_{cpr} = t_{tcp} + t_{http2} + t_{rpc} + t_{teardown}
\end{equation}

Where $t_{tcp}$ is the TCP 3-way handshake, $t_{http2}$ is the HTTP/2 SETTINGS frame negotiation, $t_{rpc}$ is the actual payload serialization/transmission, and $t_{teardown}$ is the TCP teardown (FIN/ACK). Because $t_{tcp}$ and $t_{http2}$ require multiple network round-trip times (RTTs) and significant CPU context switching, $S_{cpr} \gg t_{rpc}$.

In decentralized cloud-native architectures, the Client-side Proxy Routing (CPR) model dictates that the primary application container natively executes its own network routing and load balancing (e.g., utilizing native gRPC load balancing). This proxyless architecture minimizes intermediate network hops, optimizing baseline latency and reducing steady-state resource consumption. However, previous studies on proxyless service meshes demonstrate a critical vulnerability: the CPR model tightly couples the computational domain with the networking domain.

From a queueing theory perspective, the CPR model operates as a rigid $M/M/c$ queue. Under acute traffic surges, the network routing logic aggressively competes with the core application logic for limited CPU cycles and thread pools. When the system arrival rate approaches or exceeds the mathematical processing capacity ($\lambda \ge  \lambda_{sat}$), the application’s internal queue saturates rapidly, inevitably leading to dropped connections, unbounded tail latency, and catastrophic node failure.

\subsection{Paradigm 2: Persistent Channel Reuse (PCR)}
\label{sec2.2}

In the optimized Persistent Channel Reuse (PCR) pattern, as shown in the PCR sections of Fig. \ref{fig:seq-model} for both the synchronous and asynchronous gateway models, the architecture initializes a single, thread-safe gRPC channel during application startup. All incoming requests are multiplexed over this channel. The setup costs are amortized over millions of requests, approaching zero per request. Thus, the service time $S_{pcr}$ simplifies to:

\begin{equation}
    S_{pcr} = t_{rpc}
\end{equation}



The PCR model completely decouples the data plane from the application by offloading routing, load balancing, and telemetry to a dedicated, out-of-process sidecar proxy (Envoy). This structural shift is dynamically enacted by the autonomic controller when saturation is detected.

While conventional research often criticizes the PCR model for introducing a continuous ``service mesh tax'', permanent CPU, memory, and latency overheads during normal operations \cite{chen2025trade,zhang2026adaptive}, its architectural strength lies in robust queue management \cite{xiang2024cost}. In the PCR model, the injected sidecar acts as a highly optimized, asynchronous buffer \cite{santos2018analyzing}. It absorbs massive traffic spikes, manages connection backlogs, and applies intelligent backpressure, thereby protecting the underlying application container (whether running synchronous threads or an asynchronous event loop) from localized saturation.

\subsection{Queueing Theory and Non-Linear Saturation}
\label{sec2.3}

The catastrophic latency spikes observed in empirical testing cannot be explained by relying only on $S_{cpr}$ value. We apply an $M/M/c$ queueing model, where incoming requests arrive at rate $\lambda$ and are serviced by $c$ gateway computational resources at rate $\mu = 1/S_{gateway}$.

The utilization of the gateway system is $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{c \cdot \mu}$. According to Little's Law \cite{little2011or} and standard queueing theory, the average queuing delay $W_{queue}$ grows asymptotically as utilization $\rho \to 1$:

\begin{equation}
    W_{queue} \propto \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}
\end{equation}

Because $S_{cpr}$ is significantly larger than $S_{pcr}$, the service rate $\mu_{cpr}$ is artificially low. Therefore, under the CPR paradigm, the system reaches critical utilization ($\rho \to 1$) at a much lower arrival rate $\lambda_{sat}$. When $\lambda$ exceeds this threshold, $W_{queue}$ approaches infinity, resulting in the massive latency spikes and connection timeouts observed in our baseline data.

To fully contextualize the performance disparity between the CPR and PCR approaches, it is necessary to distinguish the roles of the TCP session, the HTTP/2 stream, and application-layer channel management. A TCP session represents the underlying Layer-4 network socket, which incurs significant latency overhead to establish. An HTTP/2 stream is a lightweight, logical sequence of frames operating within that single TCP session; this is the native mechanism gRPC uses to multiplex thousands of concurrent requests over one connection.

The fundamental issue of the CPR anti-pattern is that it actively avoids this multiplexing capability. By creating and tearing down a gRPC channel within the local request handler, CPR drives the underlying HTTP/2 library to establish and destroy a TCP session for each individual request. This limits the multiplexing capability to just one stream per connection. Contrarily, the PCR approach, enabled by the Envoy proxy, does not rebuild HTTP/2 features in the application layer. It is a structural design pattern that initializes a single, global TCP session, keeping the L4 connection persistent so that the native L7 HTTP/2 stream multiplexing functions as intended, and seamlessly absorbing the load that causes the CPR model to fail.


\subsection{Framework-Specific Queueing Dynamics}
\label{sec2.4}

While the general queueing delay $W_{queue}$ reflects system-level saturation, the structural behavior under high concurrency diverges fundamentally depending on the application framework's concurrency model. We distinguish between the multi-threaded synchronous model and the single-threaded asynchronous event-loop model.

\subsubsection{Synchronous Thread-Pool Model ($M/M/c$)}
In the synchronous architecture (e.g., Flask \cite{gunicorn}), the system relies on a thread pool of $c$ parallel worker processes. Each worker handles a single connection sequentially. When a request initiates an un-multiplexed gRPC call under the CPR paradigm, the worker thread is blocked for the entire duration of $S_{cpr}$:

\begin{equation}
	T_{\text{blocked}} = t_{tcp} + t_{http2} + t_{rpc} + t_{teardown}
\end{equation}

When the arrival rate exceeds the aggregate thread processing capacity ($\lambda \ge c\mu_{cpr}$), all $c$ workers become simultaneously occupied in I/O waiting states. Subsequent requests are diverted to the OS-level TCP listen backlog. Once this backlog fills, incoming SYN packets are dropped, driving $W_{queue} \to \infty$ and causing immediate client-side connection timeouts.

\subsubsection{Asynchronous Event-Loop Model ($M/G/1-PS$)}
In contrast, the asynchronous architecture (e.g., FastAPI \cite{uvicorn}) executes over an event loop using non-blocking I/O primitives. Rather than modeling the system as $c$ discrete servers, it is more accurately characterized as an $M/G/1$ Processor Sharing ($PS$) queue, where a single execution thread distributes CPU time across $N_{\text{active}}$ concurrent tasks \cite{dalai2024novel}.

When an asynchronous request initiates a gRPC call, it registers an I/O callback and yields control back to the event loop during the latency window $t_{tcp} + t_{http2}$. The service time distribution $G$ is general rather than exponential due to asynchronous context switching. The average queueing delay under Processor Sharing is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
	W_{queue, PS} = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} \cdot S_{g}
\end{equation}

Because the event loop does not block physical threads during network negotiation, the effective capacity limit is decoupled from OS thread count and is instead constrained by event-loop tick duration and memory overhead. This enables the asynchronous gateway to hold thousands of partially completed connections in a pending state, delaying catastrophic packet drops far beyond the limits of the synchronous $M/M/c$ model.

The Algorithm \ref{alg:ctr} is represented to accommodate mathematical processes in this section. There are four main stages in the Algorithm \ref{alg:ctr}: fetch real-time telemetry, initialization, analysis, and action stages. The fetch real-time telemetry stages will continuously accept all metrics from Prometheus required in the following process. Further acquired metric descriptions will be explained in Section \ref{sec:auto-ctr}. The initialization stages will identify which gateway framework stacks (Flask or FastAPI) the requester (frontend) uses to load the request. In the analysis stages, all metrics are evaluated using the mathematical model as discussed in this section. The results of the analysis will trigger specific steps to switch the patch, determining whether the action will be activated or not on the envoy sidecar deployment for the frontend. If the patch is considered to be deployed, this process involves evaluating the deployment time, which calculates how much time is spent on the envoy sidecar patch implementation, ensuring that the deployment responds appropriately based on the analysis outcomes.

\section{Autonomic Controller Architecture}
\label{sec:auto-ctr}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Sensing Mechanism and Telemetry Acquisition}
\label{sec3.1}
The average service time, represented as $S_{cpr}$, serves as the fundamental calculation component in establishing the mathematical saturation limit of the autonomic controller. The controller relies on two complementary Prometheus telemetry metrics to accurately calculate the $S_{cpr}$ variable in a distributed environment (e.g., RKE cluster). The controller continuously evaluates both metrics for further analysis: \textit{grpc\_client\_roundtrip\_latency\_seconds\_sum} ($gRPC_{sum}$) and \textit{grpc\_client\_roundtrip\_latency\_seconds\_count} ($gRPC_{count}$).

On the one hand, the $gRPC_{sum}$ metric serves as a continuous accumulator, gradually increasing over time. It tracks the absolute total seconds spent processing and responding to incoming remote procedure calls within the application. On the other hand, the $gRPC_{count}$ metric acts as a straightforward digital counter, registering the exact volume of discrete requests that have successfully completed their lifecycle. 

To derive the real-time average service time for the control plane, the autonomic controller calculates the quotient of the rates of change of these two metrics over a sliding observation window. Mathematically, this relationship is expressed as the ratio of their respective variations over a specific time delta $\Delta t$:

\begin{equation}
	S_{cpr} = \frac{\Delta \textit{gRPC_{sum}}_{\Delta t}}{\Delta \textit{gRPC_{count}}_{\Delta t}}
\end{equation}

In the field of classical queuing theory, service time ($S$) is commonly used to represent the duration a physical or virtual server is actively engaged in a single unit of work. By utilizing native gRPC roundtrip metrics, the controller captures highly accurate, comprehensive processing measurements instead of isolated internal application timers. This duration inherently includes data serialization, framework event-loop overhead, and the core CPU execution required for the prime number calculations. These processes are ilustrated in the Fig. \ref{fig:ctr-arc}. 



\subsection{Calculation and Analysis}
\label{sec3.2}

\subsubsection{Predictive Mathematical Formulation}
To mathematically formalize the system's performance under increasing target rates, the system architecture is modeled as an $M/M/c$ queue. This assumes a Poisson arrival process for incoming requests with rate $\lambda$ (target rate), exponentially distributed service times with rate $\mu$ (where $\mu = 1 / S_{cpr}$), and $c$ parallel computing resources \cite{tijms2003first} \cite{Banerjee2023MTDDHJSMTA}. For the system to remain stable, utilization must remain below capacity ($\rho < 1$). As the target rate $\lambda$ increases toward the total service capacity $c\mu$, the system enters a state of high utilization. According to Erlang's C formula \cite{angus2001introduction}, the probability that an arriving request must wait in the queue ($P_Q$) is:

\begin{equation}
	P_Q = \frac{\frac{(c\rho)^c}{c!(1-\rho)}}{\sum_{n=0}^{c-1} \frac{(c\rho)^n}{n!} + \frac{(c\rho)^c}{c!(1-\rho)}}
\end{equation}

Consequently, the average expected wait time in the queue ($W_{queue}$) grows non-linearly:

\begin{equation}
	W_{queue} = \frac{P_Q}{c\mu(1-\rho)}
\end{equation}

As $\rho \to 1$, $W_{queue}$ approaches infinity. This mathematical phenomenon explains the severe spikes observed in empirical $p99$ tail latencies. Rather than waiting for system utilization to completely fail ($\rho \ge 1.0$), the controller continuously polls real-time telemetry to compute the empirical service time ($S_{cpr}$) and calculates the mathematical saturation limit ($\lambda_{sat}$) based on the application's theoretical concurrency ($c$):

\begin{equation}
	\lambda_{sat} = \frac{c}{S_{cpr}}
\end{equation}

To guarantee survivability, the controller defines a predictive intervention boundary ($\lambda_{trigger}$) using a configurable safety threshold ratio ($T_{ratio}$):

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:trigger}
	\lambda_{trigger} = \lambda_{sat} \times T_{ratio}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{SLA Evaluation and Bottleneck Verification} 
While the predictive trigger anticipates resource exhaustion using theoretical capacity as stated in \eqref{eq:trigger}, we also include a critical empirical verification of actual system degradation to avoid any impulsive actuation. The controller achieves this through real-time Service Level Agreement (SLA) monitoring, specifically targeting the 95th percentile application latency ($L_{p95}$). To acquire this metric accurately, the control plane queries the \textit{grpc\_client\_roundtrip\_latency\_\break seconds\_bucket} histogram exposed directly by the application runtime. By calculating the 95th quantile from the rate of change across these distribution buckets over a one-minute sliding window, the system extracts a stabilized, real-time measurement of the tail latency. We rely on this internal gRPC telemetry rather than the external HTTP network timers. It strictly separates the application's true compute and event-loop processing delays from external network issues, such as TCP backlog saturation or service queuing in the service mesh.

Targeting the 95th percentile ensures the controller focuses on the degraded experience of the slowest requests while filtering out extreme, transient outliers (like the 99th percentile) that are often caused by routine garbage collection or minor operating system context switching. This observed $L_{p95}$ value is continuously evaluated against a rigid, configurable latency boundary ($L_{SLA}$, set to $35$ ms). Within the hybrid controller mode, this SLA evaluation acts as the definitive proof of exhaustion. The mathematical capacity limit ($\lambda_{trigger}$) flags that the system should be saturated based on average service times, but the $L_{p95}$ breach provides the empirical confirmation that the synchronous thread pool or asynchronous event loop has actually failed to process the current concurrent workload. By requiring this latency condition, the controller completely avoids false-positive mitigations during brief, harmless traffic bursts.



\subsection{Actuation and Dynamic Cutover}
\label{sec3.3}

The control framework directly solves architectural inelasticity by bridging the CPR and PCR states dynamically. To prevent false positives caused by transient network jitter, the controller implements a strict dual-condition actuation gate. The dynamic cutover is only authorized if the predictive mathematical limit is breached \textit{and} the empirical SLA is actively failing (exceeding the $35$ ms threshold). The final condition is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\text{Trigger} \rightarrow \text{True} \quad \text{if} \quad (\lambda \ge \lambda_{trigger}) \land (L_{p95} > 35\text{ ms})
\end{equation}

Upon satisfying this dual-condition logic, the autonomic controller intervenes proactively. It initiates a dynamic patch to the frontend deployment within the Kubernetes control plane, applying the \textit{sidecar.istio.io/inject="true"} annotation. This action forces a rolling restart, attaching the Envoy proxy to the workload in real-time. By dynamically shifting from a low-overhead CPR topology to a highly resilient PCR topology exclusively when these two thresholds intersect, the controller caps the connection bottleneck and neutralizes exponential queuing delay before catastrophic SLA violations occur.

\subsubsection{End-to-end Comprehensive Evaluation}
To evaluate the overall system performance and comprehensive user experience of the deployed microservices architecture, an end-to-end (E2E) evaluation stage was conducted. This scenario grabs the entire round-trip request lifecycle, effectively capturing the client’s perception of performance as the traffic flows through the external network. Using \textit{wrk2} as a load generator for a highly efficient HTTP benchmarking tool, we conduct the penetration test of the cluster via the frontend ingress, translate from HTTP to gRPC, process the request at the backend, and return the response to the client. This evaluation scenario is shown in the Fig. \ref{fig:eval}a.

The frontend serves as an API gateway, handling standard HTTP requests, transform the payload into gRPC protocol buffers, and routing the traffic to the backend prime-server-service. This topology ensures that the evaluation captures all systemic overhead within a 120-second time frame in 30 runs for each payload, including HTTP/gRPC translation costs, cluster network mesh latency, and the total resource usage of both frontend and backend components. 

In this scenario, we also evaluate the system's adaptability, the framework efficiency, and the control logic. To obtain the E2E evaluation result, we incorporate interchangeable frontend implementations and multiple operational states. The API gateway tier was interchangeably evaluated using Flask (representing a traditional synchronous Web Server Gateway Interface-WSGI \cite{wsgi} handling profile) and FastAPI (representing a modern asynchronous Asynchronous Server Gateway Interface-ASGI \cite{asgi} handling profile) to measure HTTP-to-gRPC translation overhead. Evaluations were strictly segmented between a Baseline state (relying entirely on default Kubernetes static scheduling) and a Controller-enabled state (where the custom dynamic control loop actively managed the system). During both Baseline state and Controller-enabled evaluations, the system was independently tested in CPR and PCR modes to determine their respective impacts on latency mitigation, resource scaling, and total end-to-end throughput under varying load intensities.

To maintain rigorous evaluation control and eliminate state leakage between load phases, each evaluation cycle executed an automated pipeline:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{System Provisioning:} First, we ensure that all CPUs on the SUT node are in high-performance mode and remain in that state for further processes. Both the frontend and backend Kubernetes deployments and their associated services were dynamically applied. The script paused execution until all pods across both tiers achieved a Ready state.
	
	\item \textbf{Endpoint Resolution:} The external IP or NodePort of the Frontend service was dynamically resolved to establish the absolute target URL for the \textit{wrk} client.
	
	\item \textbf{Comprehensive Telemetry Sampling:} A background daemon initiated continuous polling of container-level resource consumption using the Kubernetes Metrics API. This process simultaneously recorded CPU utilization (m) and memory usage (Mi) for frontend pods at 2-second intervals. This allowed us to conduct a comparative analysis of resource bottlenecks across the service mesh, whenever the current system is running with or without the controller.
	
	\item \textbf{Traffic Injection:} The \textit{wrk} benchmarking tool injected sustained HTTP payloads for the designated duration (120 seconds) at the specified rate and concurrency.
	
	\item \textbf{Data Processing and Metric Extraction:} Upon completion of the injection phase, the script parsed the wrk outputs to extract the true client-facing metrics, include actual RPS ($R_{act}$), total Round-Trip time ($T_{RTT}$), latency distribution ($p50, p99$), and resources utilization (CPU and Memory).
	
	\item \textbf{Environment Teardown:} All deployments and services were completely decommissioned. The script enforced a mandatory cooldown period, monitoring the namespace until all resources were fully terminated before initiating the subsequent test iteration.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results and Performance Analysis}

The evaluation results demonstrate that asymptotic latency degradation in the microservices architecture originates directly from the queueing constraints imposed by the Connection-Per-Request (CPR) paradigm. Conversely, implementing Persistent Channel Reuse (PCR)—either statically or dynamically via our proposed autonomic controller—fundamentally alters this queueing behavior, preventing premature queue saturation and protecting client-facing service level agreements (SLAs).

\subsection{Isolated Backend Microservice Benchmark}
\label{sec5.1}

To establish a strict computational baseline and isolate the source of system-wide bottlenecks, the backend prime-checking service was evaluated independently using the \textit{ghz} load testing tool over an isolated internal cluster interface.

Across the entire load range (200 to 1200 RPS across 30 independent iterations per load tier), the backend pods consistently delivered a total average response latency ranging from $0.2\text{ ms}$ at 200 RPS to $0.29\text{ ms}$ at 1200 RPS, as shown in the Fig. \ref{fig:baseline-backend}a. The Fig. \ref{fig:baseline-backend}b shows the aggregated observed telemetry results revealing that the pure algorithm execution time ($T_{\text{compute}}$) remained negligible and virtually static between $0.002\text{ ms}$ and $0.003\text{ ms}$. Over 98.9\% of the backend response was consumed by internal cluster network propagation and lightweight gRPC queueing ($T_{\text{net\_queue}} \approx 0.19\text{ ms } \text{to } 0.29\text{ ms}$). Furthermore, tail latencies remained particularly stable, with $P_{99}$ latency increasing from $0.28\text{ ms}$ at 200 RPS to a maximum of $0.54\text{ ms}$ at 1200 RPS.

The backend successfully processed 100\% of the injected traffic without a single connection drop or timeout. At the maximum tested load of 1200 RPS, the system maintained an actual throughput of 1199.87 RPS—as shown in the Fig. \ref{fig:baseline-backend}c—proving that the underlying mathematical service operates far below its saturation threshold. Nevertheless, Fig. \ref{fig:baseline-backend}d shows that resource monitoring confirmed predictable, linear scaling. CPU consumption increased proportionally from $83.51\text{ millicores}$ at 200 RPS to $281.16\text{ millicores}$ at 1200 RPS, while memory footprint remained flat at approximately $200\text{ MiB}$. This flat latency and resource profile demonstrates that the core backend logic maintains computational capacity well beyond the maximum injected load rate, isolating all systemic performance degradation strictly to the API gateway tier.

\subsection{End-to-End System Evaluation: CPR vs. PCR Baseline}
\label{sec5.2}

Evaluating the complete end-to-end (E2E) workflow via HTTP traffic injected by \textit{wrk2} revealed severe performance disparities between connection paradigms and gateway framework architectures, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:baseline-comp}.

Under the Connection-Per-Request (CPR) paradigm, both synchronous (Flask) and asynchronous (FastAPI) gateways hit critical queue saturation ($\rho \to 1$) at moderate load thresholds. For Flask CPR, increasing load from 200 RPS to 1000 RPS caused average network and queueing latency ($T_{\text{net}}$) to explode from $4.98\text{ ms}$ to $20.45\text{ s}$, accompanied by a CPU utilization spike to $1318.9\text{ millicores}$. As illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:seq-model}, this catastrophic failure occurs because worker threads and event loops become completely blocked managing repetitive L4 TCP handshakes and L7 HTTP/2 frame negotiations ($S_{cpr}$), exhausting local listen backlogs. The throughput degradation of both FastAPI and Flask CPR was recorded at approximately 670 RPS, as shown in the Fig. \ref{fig:baseline-perf}c. This proves that when the incoming Request-per-Second (RPS) rate exceeds the maximum processing capacity ($\mu$) of a server with fixed resource constraints, throughput does not just flat at maximum capacity; rather, it rapidly dives toward zero while response latency approaches infinity. 

Conversely, enforcing Persistent Channel Reuse (PCR) qualitatively transformed system stability. These phenomena are clearly shown in the Fig. \ref{fig:baseline-perf}. By multiplexing concurrent requests over persistent gRPC channels, Flask PCR reduced $T_{\text{net}}$ at 1000 RPS to $36.31\text{ ms}$ (a 99.8\% reduction compared to CPR) while lowering the CPU footprint to $365.2\text{ millicores}$. FastAPI PCR demonstrated superior concurrency handling, maintaining a $T_{\text{net}}$ of only $5.71\text{ ms}$ at 1000 RPS with a minimal CPU footprint of $252.4\text{ millicores}$.

\subsection{Comparative Efficacy Under Persistent Connection Reuse (PCR) Loads}
\label{sec5.3_pcr_comparison}

While the Connection Per-Request (CPR) anti-pattern demonstrates universal vulnerability to TCP connection churn, evaluating the Persistent Connection Reuse (PCR) profile reveals fundamental structural differences between synchronous (Flask) and asynchronous (FastAPI) runtime architectures. Under PCR, gRPC channels are initialized at application startup and multiplexed across requests, eliminating $t_{\text{tcp}}$ and $t_{\text{http2}}$ setup overheads per transaction.

\subsubsection{Synchronous Framework (Flask) PCR Analysis}
In the synchronous WSGI model, keeping downstream gRPC channels persistent drastically increases service rate ($\mu_{\text{pcr}} \gg \mu_{\text{cpr}}$). As shown in Table~\ref{tab:compare-analysis}, at light-to-moderate load tiers (200--600 RPS), Flask PCR maintains a flat latency profile with $P_{50} \le 1.07\text{ ms}$ and $P_{99} \le 64.56\text{ ms}$. However, as traffic scales to heavy load tiers (1100--1200 RPS), physical worker thread pool constraints ($c$ workers) reintroduce queueing backlog ($W_{\text{queue}}$). At 1200 RPS, the unmanaged Flask PCR baseline experiences latency inflation, reaching a $P_{50}$ of $385.09\text{ ms}$ and a $P_{99}$ of $1737.32\text{ ms}$ with an actual throughput of $1192.76\text{ RPS}$.

Upon reaching the controller's intervention threshold ($\lambda_{\text{trigger}} = 186.1\text{ RPS}$, $L_{p95} \ge 50\text{ ms}$), the autonomic controller executes sidecar cutover in $\Delta T_{\text{rollout}} = 0.74\text{ s}$. Post-actuation, Envoy's out-of-process buffer absorbs connection bursts and enforces asynchronous backpressure. At 1200 RPS, controller-actuated Flask PCR restores throughput to a full $1,200.18\text{ RPS}$ ($+0.6\%$ gain over baseline), while suppressing $P_{50}$ latency by $59.8\%$ ($385.09\text{ ms} \to 154.62\text{ ms}$) and $P_{99}$ tail latency by $73.4\%$ ($1,737.32\text{ ms} \to 462.13\text{ ms}$).

\subsubsection{Asynchronous Framework (FastAPI) PCR Analysis}
The combination of an asynchronous event loop ($M/G/1-PS$ queueing model) and Persistent Connection Reuse (PCR) achieves optimal architectural efficiency. Across the entire load spectrum (200--1200 RPS), FastAPI PCR processes 100\% of injected traffic without any queue degradation. At the maximum load of 1200 RPS, actual throughput reaches $1,201.78\text{ RPS}$ with an average latency of only $10.05\text{ ms}$, a $P_{50}$ latency of $1.41\text{ ms}$, and a $P_{99}$ tail latency of $91.19\text{ ms}$.

Significantly as shown in Table \ref{tab:controller-telemetry-summary}, across all test runs for FastAPI PCR, the autonomic controller's evaluation loop returned \texttt{triggered = False} (Never Triggered), $\Delta T_{rollout} (\text{s}) = 0.0$. Because the non-blocking event loop paired with persistent HTTP/2 stream multiplexing kept tail latencies well below $L_{\text{SLA}} = 35\text{ ms}$, the predictive controller correctly refrained from actuating. This zero-false-positive outcome empirically validates the precision of Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctr}: it actively intervenes during saturation events while avoiding unnecessary sidecar memory and CPU overheads when the underlying stack operates within healthy SLA bounds.

\subsubsection{The Inherent Gateway Transcoding Tax}

This empirical gap highlights a critical \textit{Performance-Abstraction Paradox}: although API gateways provide essential protocol decoupling, their internal serialization frameworks establish an absolute minimum service time ($S_{\text{transcode}}$) that vastly exceeds pure backend algorithm execution ($T_{\text{compute}} \approx 0.002\text{ ms}$).

\subsubsection{Framework-Specific Queueing Degradation}
The operational impact of this fixed transcoding tax becomes critical when mapped to queueing models, as it dictates the gateway's baseline service rate ($\mu = 1/S$). 

In the synchronous multi-threaded architecture (Flask), request processing operates as an $M/M/c$ queue where worker threads block during both CPU transcoding and downstream network I/O. Under Connection Per-Request (CPR), connection negotiation ($t_{\text{tcp}} + t_{\text{http2}}$) inflates total service time ($S_{\text{cpr}}$), severely depressing service rate $\mu_{\text{cpr}}$. As arrival rate $\lambda$ approaches total worker capacity $c\mu_{\text{cpr}}$, utilization $\rho = \frac{\lambda}{c\mu} \to 1$. Governed by Little's Law \cite{little2011or}, queueing delay $W_{\text{queue}} \propto \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}$ grows asymptotically, driving OS listen backlogs to saturation and causing severe packet drops.

In contrast, the asynchronous event-loop architecture (FastAPI) operates as an $M/G/1$ Processor Sharing ($PS$) queue \cite{KahramanMG1,dalai2024novel}. By yielding execution during external gRPC I/O wait states, FastAPI decouples task handling from OS physical thread counts, distributing CPU tick duration across $N_{\text{active}}$ concurrent connections:
\begin{equation}
	W_{\text{queue, PS}} = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} \cdot S_{g}
\end{equation}
Because the single execution thread is not blocked by network handshakes, FastAPI's service time remains closer to the pure CPU transcoding tax, delaying catastrophic queue collapse to higher arrival rates compared to synchronous thread pools.

\subsubsection{Real-Time Controller Telemetry Observability}
To prevent queue collapse without maintaining static sidecars during low-traffic periods, Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctr} employs a continuous telemetry loop that operationalizes the $M/M/c$ and $M/G/1-PS$ queueing equations. These phenomena result in graphs as shown in the Fig. \ref{fig:eval-ctr}. At polling intervals ($t_{\text{poll}}$), the controller queries Prometheus to extract real-time metrics and compute theoretical saturation boundaries:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Real-Time Parameterization:} The telemetry engine continuously records the arrival rate $\lambda$ ($controller\_rps$) and service time $S$ ($service\_time\_ms$).
	\item \textbf{Saturation \& Intervention Thresholds:} Using $S$, the controller dynamically computes the predicted crash point $\lambda_{\text{sat}} = \frac{c}{S}$ ($crash\_point\_rps$) and the predictive trigger threshold $\lambda_{\text{trigger}} = \lambda_{\text{sat}} \times \tau_{\text{ratio}}$ ($trigger\_point\_rps$), where target utilization ratio $\tau_{\text{ratio}} = 0.80$.
	\item \textbf{Dual-Condition Actuation:} Sidecar injection occurs if and only if predicted saturation coincides with SLA degradation ($\lambda \ge \lambda_{\text{trigger}} \;\land\; L_{p95} \ge L_{\text{SLA}}$).
\end{enumerate}

As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:controller-telemetry-summary}, empirical telemetry confirms the accuracy of the mathematical formulation across all four operational modes:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Sync (Flask) CPR Telemetry:} Under saturated load ($\ge 600\text{ RPS}$), connection churn depresses the average service rate, resulting in an observed service time $S = 9.93\text{ ms}$. This drops the calculated saturation threshold to $\lambda_{\text{sat}} = 103.96\text{ RPS}$ and $\lambda_{\text{trigger}} = 83.17\text{ RPS}$. Because target load rates ($\lambda \ge 600\text{ RPS}$) far exceed $\lambda_{\text{trigger}}$, the controller achieves a 100\% actuation rate (210/210 runs), executing sidecar injection in $\Delta T_{\text{rollout}} = 8.32\text{ s}$.
	
	\item \textbf{Async (FastAPI) CPR Telemetry:} Under high CPR load, FastAPI exhibits $S = 9.86\text{ ms}$, yielding $\lambda_{\text{sat}} = 210.41\text{ RPS}$ and $\lambda_{\text{trigger}} = 168.33\text{ RPS}$. While non-blocking execution doubles the effective saturation limit relative to Flask CPR, loads $\ge 600\text{ RPS}$ still exceed capacity, prompting a 100\% actuation rate with $\Delta T_{\text{rollout}} = 8.27\text{ s}$.
	
	\item \textbf{Sync (Flask) PCR Telemetry:} Channel reuse reduces service time to $S = 5.51\text{ ms}$ under heavy load, raising predicted capacity to $\lambda_{\text{sat}} = 186.09\text{ RPS}$ ($\lambda_{\text{trigger}} = 148.87\text{ RPS}$). Sidecar injection is triggered selectively (5.2\% of runs) only when transient thread contention causes $L_{p95} \ge L_{\text{SLA}}$ at peak loads, completing rollout in $\Delta T_{\text{rollout}} = 7.85\text{ s}$.
	
	\item \textbf{Async (FastAPI) PCR Telemetry:} Multiplexed async execution maintains $S = 2.04\text{ ms}$ even at 1200 RPS, keeping predicted capacity high ($\lambda_{\text{sat}} = 1050.32\text{ RPS}$) and $P_{95}$ latency well below $L_{\text{SLA}} = 35\text{ ms}$. Consequently, the controller records an actuation rate of 0.0\% (0/210 runs), confirming zero false-positive deployments.
	
	\item \textbf{Daemon Resource Footprint:} Across all 1320 total test iterations, the controller daemon operated with negligible resource overhead, consuming an average CPU footprint of $8.28\text{ millicores } \text{to } 9.32\text{ millicores}$ ($< 1\%$ of a single CPU core) and maintaining a static memory footprint of $56.59\text{ MiB } \text{to } 56.78\text{ MiB}$.
\end{itemize}

Ultimately, transitioning the system to PCR—either statically or via dynamic Envoy sidecar actuation—scales effective service rate $\mu_{\text{pcr}} \gg \mu_{\text{cpr}}$, keeping system utilization $\rho \ll 1$ and completely suppressing non-linear latency spikes without requiring additional hardware provisioning.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The performance evaluation at the gateway level in this paper shows the reality of modern cloud-native system design. While microservice architectures enable strict decoupling and flexibility in protocol, the latency tax associated with these abstractions is seldom investigated in detail at the millisecond level. The resulting data bridges theoretical queueing limits with practical operational constraints, offering three primary insights for industry practitioners.

\subsubsection{The Microservice Abstraction Penalty}
A striking finding from our compositional analysis is the significant disparity between the transcoding protocol overhead and the pure computational logic execution. At the API gateway tier, this factor accounts for $90\%$ to $97.5\%$ of the total end-to-end latency. Our empirical data analysis confirms that the structural bottleneck in modern web services no longer lies in raw network transmission or database I/O, but rather in the CPU-bound processes of payload serialization and deserialization.

This aligns closely with Barroso \emph{et al.}'s observations, in which microsecond-scale overheads in remote procedure calls (RPCs) and protocol parsing increasingly dominate datacenter latency \cite{barroso2017attack}. Furthermore, research surrounding the DeathStarBench microservice suite (Gan \emph{et al.}) highlights that as applications become hyper-fragmented, the cumulative cost of proxying and translating payloads (e.g., JSON to Protocol Buffers) can exceed the execution time of the microservices themselves \cite{gan2019deathstarbench}. The \textit{Inherent Proxy Latency Gap} quantified in this study underscores a Performance-Abstraction Paradox: while API gateways are mandatory for external security and routing, their static overhead ($S_{\text{transcode}}$) establishes a hard performance floor that cannot be bypassed by backend optimizations.

\subsubsection{The Thread Pool Trap}
When synchronous web architectures (such as Flask) face sudden traffic spikes, the default industry response is vertical scaling—provisioning nodes with higher CPU core counts ($c$). However, our queueing analysis of the Connection Per-Request (CPR) paradigm demonstrates why this approach is economically and computationally flawed.

Recent cloud and n-tier application work shows that these software limits must be managed together with hardware scaling. In an $M/M/c$ queueing environment, worker threads are completely blocked during external network operations ($t_{\text{tcp}} + t_{\text{http2}}$). Carnevali \emph{et al.} summarize that both overprovisioning and underprovisioning can be addressed by coordinating hardware and software resources such as threads and database connections, because mismatched settings create inefficiencies in concurrent request processing across application tiers \cite{carnevali2025compositional}. Our empirical data proves that under CPR, the service time ($S_{\text{cpr}}$) is inflated not by CPU utilization, but by I/O wait states. Consequently, adding more CPU cores fails to increase the effective service rate $\mu$; it merely results in more threads remaining idle. This \textit{Thread Pool Trap} necessitates a shift away from vertical scaling and toward persistent connection multiplexing, breaking the dependency between physical CPU cores and concurrent network connections.

\subsubsection{Autonomic Mitigation vs. Architectural Cure}
Addressing the aforementioned bottlenecks requires in outlining between immediate operational mitigation and long-term architectural cures. As demonstrated in this paper, migrating to an asynchronous, event-loop framework (FastAPI) acts as the ultimate architectural cure. By transforming the system into an $M/G/1$ Processor Sharing ($PS$) queue, the application decouples task execution from connection wait states and natively supports high arrival rates ($\lambda$) without sidecar intervention.

However, enterprise production cloud-native environments are heavily burdened with legacy synchronous microservices that cannot be rapidly refactored. In these scenarios, the autonomic controller developed in this study serves as a critical stabilization mechanism. This modeling choice matters directly for refactoring and runtime control. Rooted in Kephart and Chess’s vision of autonomic computing \cite{kephart2003vision} (the MAPE-K loop: Monitor, Analyze, Plan, Execute \cite{armstrong2017towards}), the controller transforms static queueing theory into a dynamic telemetry guardrail. By injecting an Envoy sidecar only when real-time utilization ($\rho$) threatens saturation, the system achieves the connection pooling benefits of a service mesh without suffering the permanent CPU/memory footprint of a ubiquitous sidecar deployment. Ultimately, while asynchronous application refactoring is the definitive cure, precision-targeted autonomic injection provides an essential, zero-waste insurance policy for vulnerable synchronous infrastructures.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclude}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In this paper, we investigated the hidden performance costs of application-layer communication bottlenecks in modern microservice architectures, focusing specifically on the catastrophic latency degradation caused by gRPC connection churn within API gateways. By modeling the Connection-Per-Request (CPR) anti-pattern and contrasting it with a refactored Persistent Connection Reuse (PCR) paradigm, we exposed the structural limitations of standard gateway implementations. Our evaluation, conducted across both synchronous (Flask, $M/M/c$ queueing) and asynchronous (FastAPI, $M/G/1-PS$ queueing) frameworks within an isolated Kubernetes cluster across load tiers from 200 to 1200 RPS, demonstrated that mismanaged protocol abstractions inherently dictate system scalability boundaries. 

Our empirical analysis results conclusively showed that transitioning from CPR to PCR significantly reduced the connection setup overheads ($t_{\text{tcp}} + t_{\text{http2}}$) and prevents asymptotic queue collapse. Under a peak load of 1000 RPS, adopting PCR reduced network/queueing delay ($T_{\text{net}}$) by $99.82\%$ for Flask ($20454.8\text{ ms} \to 36.31\text{ ms}$) and by $99.97\%$ for FastAPI ($20518.2\text{ ms} \to 5.71\text{ ms}$). At the maximum load tier of 1200 RPS—where unmanaged CPR baselines suffered a $43.5\%$ throughput collapse and median latencies exceeding $27000\text{ ms}$—FastAPI PCR maintained a full $1201.78\text{ RPS}$ throughput with a median ($P_{50}$) latency of just $1.41\text{ ms}$ (a $99.99\%$ reduction relative to CPR). 

Furthermore, for legacy architectures where application refactoring is unfeasible, our zero-waste autonomic Envoy controller demonstrated remarkable mitigation efficacy. By continually tracking real-time utilization ($\rho$) via Prometheus telemetry, the controller accurately predicts queue saturation ($\lambda \ge \lambda_{\text{trigger}}$) and dynamically injects Envoy sidecar proxies upon SLA violation ($L_{p95} \ge L_{\text{SLA}}$). Under saturated CPR conditions at 1200 RPS, controller actuation increased throughput by $+63.2\%$ for Flask ($678.6 \to 1107.8\text{ RPS}$) and by $+60.5\%$ for FastAPI ($680.3 \to 1092.1\text{ RPS}$), while suppressing $P_{99}$ tail latencies by up to $79.9\%$. Crucially, the controller recorded a $0.0\%$ false-positive actuation rate during performant runs (such as FastAPI PCR), operating with a minimal daemon resource footprint ($< 9.5\text{ m}$ CPU, $56.7\text{ MiB}$ RAM). 

Future work will expand upon this benchmarking methodology in two distinct directions. First, we will transition the evaluation into the domain of Chaos Engineering by introducing controlled infrastructure faults (e.g., node CPU starvation, transient network partitioning, and unexpected pod termination) under sustained load to measure the resilience, cutover latency, and recovery dynamics of the autonomic controller. Second, building upon our findings regarding the \textit{Thread Pool Trap}—which proved that vertical scaling (expanding physical CPU core capacity $c$) fails to resolve I/O-bound connection queues in synchronous systems—future research will examine horizontal scalability. We aim to integrate Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaling (HPA) alongside our autonomic controller to analyze how dynamic pod replica scaling interacts with targeted sidecar injection. This will offer deeper insights into resolving transient gateway bottlenecks through horizontal load redistribution rather than localized resource expansion, moving toward a fully self-healing, SLA-aware cloud-native ingress architecture.
